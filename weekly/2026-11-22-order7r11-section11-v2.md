::: {custom-style="Synopsis"}
**Synopsis.** Applications for rejection of a plaint and pleas of res judicata are routinely joined, and routinely confused. This note examines the scope of enquiry under Order VII Rule 11, the operation of Section 11 and its constructive limb, the point at which the two provisions meet, and the Supreme Court and Gujarat High Court decisions of 2026 that bear on the trial court's practice. It concludes with a working procedure for the defendant, the plaintiff and the court.
:::

# 1. Introduction

An application under Order VII Rule 11 is the least expensive weapon in a defendant's armoury, yet it can prove one of the costliest when it fails, since it takes only a few pages to file but, because the order on it will usually travel to the High Court in revision or appeal whichever way it goes, it can consume a year of the suit's life; and because the plea of res judicata under Section 11 is the objection most frequently joined to it, the two are pleaded together as a matter of habit and, as a consequence, are often confused with one another.

That confusion is avoidable, for the provisions answer different questions: Order VII Rule 11 asks whether the plaint, read by itself, discloses a suit that the law permits the court to try, whereas Section 11 asks whether the matter has already been decided between the same parties, so that the first question can be answered from a single document while the second cannot be answered without the record of another proceeding. Much of the Supreme Court's work in this area, including decisions delivered in April, June and November of this year, consists of keeping the two questions apart.

Against that background, this note sets out the settled law on each provision and considers the point at which they meet, then examines the decisions of 2026 that matter most to a trial court and, finally, proposes a working procedure for the advocate who must either invoke these provisions or defeat them.

# 2. Order VII Rule 11: The Scope of Enquiry

Rule 11 directs the court to reject a plaint in six situations, namely where it discloses no cause of action, where the relief is undervalued and the valuation is not corrected within the time allowed, where the plaint is written on insufficiently stamped paper and the deficiency is not made good, where the suit appears from the statement in the plaint to be barred by any law, where the plaint is not filed in duplicate, and where the plaintiff fails to comply with Rule 9, although in practice clauses (a) and (d) account for almost all of the litigation that arises under the Rule.

## 2.1 The defence is excluded

The starting point is *Saleem Bhai v. State of Maharashtra*,[^1] which holds that the court looks to the averments in the plaint and to nothing else, so that the written statement and the defendant's version of events are irrelevant at this stage, and the principles were later consolidated in *Dahiben v. Arvindbhai Kalyanji Bhanusali (Gajra)*,[^2] where the Supreme Court emphasised that the power is a drastic one whose conditions must be strictly observed, that the documents filed with the plaint are read along with it, and that a document on which the plaint is founded forms part of it. The test, accordingly, is whether a decree would follow if the averments were taken as true in their entirety together with those documents, and where the plaint discloses a real cause of action the court does not go on to inquire whether the allegations are in fact true.

## 2.2 The reading must be meaningful

That said, the exercise is not a mechanical one, for in *T. Arivandandam v. T.V. Satyapal* Krishna Iyer J. directed trial courts to read the plaint meaningfully and not formally, and observed that where clever drafting has created only the illusion of a cause of action the court should nip the suit in the bud at the first hearing, examining the party under Order X where necessary,[^3] a caution that *Dahiben* repeats in adding that the court must remain alive to camouflage and to the suppression of facts which would expose a bar.[^4]

## 2.3 The plaint is rejected as a whole or not at all

Furthermore, because the plaint is read as a whole, and passages are not to be culled out and read in isolation,[^5] it follows that a plaint cannot be rejected against some defendants or in respect of some reliefs while being retained for the rest, which is why the Supreme Court in *Sejal Glass Ltd. v. Navilan Merchants (P) Ltd.* set aside a High Court order that had struck the plaint down as against the directors but kept it alive against the company,[^6] and why the same rule was applied again in *Madhav Prasad Aggarwal v. Axis Bank Ltd.*[^7] The tactical consequence is plain, since in a suit with several defendants or reliefs an application under Rule 11 can succeed only if the objection reaches every one of them.

## 2.4 Limitation must appear on the face of the plaint

Clause (d) speaks of a suit barred by law "as appears from the statement in the plaint", and limitation therefore falls within it only where the plaint and its annexures establish the bar without the aid of evidence, a point illustrated by *N. Asha Devi v. R. Aravind Kumar*, decided on 17 August 2026,[^9] in which the plaintiff-developer had sued in October 2022 on joint venture agreements of August 2014, although the plaint and its annexures showed that the last relevant communication between the parties dated from November 2016 and that later notices could not create a fresh cause of action. Relying on *Shri Mukund Bhavan Trust v. Shrimant Chhatrapati Udayan Raje Pratapsinh Maharaj Bhonsle*,[^10] the Supreme Court reversed the trial court and the High Court and rejected the plaint, observing that a court should not hesitate to do so when the bar is obvious from the pleadings themselves.

The position is quite different, however, where limitation depends on a disputed fact, because if the plaintiff pleads that the date of his knowledge was late and ties that plea to fraud, the question is ordinarily a mixed question of fact and law which must be tried and cannot be decided summarily, and the two lines of authority are reconciled by the source of the facts: when the facts that fix the starting point of limitation are admitted or apparent from the plaint and its documents, clause (d) applies, but when they are contested, it does not.

## 2.5 Stage: "at any stage" does not mean "at any time"

It is well settled that the power may be exercised at any stage of the suit, whether before registration, after summons, or before the trial concludes,[^11] but the Gujarat High Court has now drawn a practical limit, for in *Nirav Madhukar Buch v. Lilaben Bhikhubhai Thakor*, reported on 7 September 2026, the defendant had applied under Rule 11 on 27 October 2025, by which time the plaintiff's oral evidence had closed and the defendant's own evidence was being recorded.[^12] Shelat J. upheld the order of the Principal Senior Civil Judge, Valsad, refusing the application, holding that the right to apply at any stage does not extend to the fag end of the trial and that such applications should be rejected with costs, and adding that reliance on admissions elicited in cross-examination falls outside Rule 11 altogether, since only the plaint may be examined; limitation was already a framed issue that would be decided along with the others, and the revision was accordingly dismissed with costs of Rs. 10,000.

## 2.6 Rejection is not always final

Finally, under Order VII Rule 13 the rejection of a plaint does not of its own force prevent the plaintiff from presenting a fresh plaint on the same cause of action, although where the ground of rejection was limitation the point is largely academic, since the fresh suit would face the same bar.

# 3. Section 11 and Constructive Res Judicata

Section 11 bars a court from trying a suit or an issue in which the matter directly and substantially in issue has been directly and substantially in issue in a former suit between the same parties, or parties under whom they claim, litigating under the same title, in a court competent to try the later suit, and has been heard and finally decided, while Explanation IV extends the bar to "any matter which might and ought to have been made ground of defence or attack" in the former suit, which is deemed to have been directly and substantially in issue.

The doctrine rests on public policy, namely the finality of litigation and the protection of a party from being vexed twice for the same cause,[^13] but because it is a rule of procedure and not of substantive law it has recognised limits, so that, for instance, a decision on a pure question of law, unrelated to the rights of the parties in the earlier suit, does not bind them in a later one,[^14] and, equally, constructive res judicata is not confined to suits but has been applied to writ proceedings and to public interest litigation as well.[^15] Three decisions of 2026 show how the "might and ought" test is applied, and taken together they offer a useful guide to the likely outcome in a given case.

## 3.1 *Channappa v. Parvatewwa*

On 9 April 2026 a bench of Dipankar Datta and Augustine George Masih JJ. held that a later title suit was barred by constructive res judicata because title had been in dispute in the earlier proceedings,[^16] the reasoning being that a party who had occasion to assert title and did not do so cannot assert it in a second suit.

## 3.2 *Makardhwaj Ram v. Jagdish Rai*

On different facts, however, Sanjay Karol and N. Kotiswar Singh JJ. reached the opposite result on 11 June 2026,[^17] declining to apply Explanation IV mechanically and holding that constructive res judicata turns on the facts of each case, so that a party is not required to litigate in earlier proceedings a right that was not then put in question.

## 3.3 *Ashok Kumar v. Central Bank of India*

Most recently, on 18 November 2026, Dipankar Datta and Sheel Nagu JJ. held that constructive res judicata cannot bar a claim whose cause of action had not arisen when the earlier proceeding began,[^18] since a retired employee's claim for interest on salary arrears had crystallised only when the arrears were paid, years after the earlier decision, and was therefore a fresh cause of action; even so, the Court upheld the dismissal of the miscellaneous application by which he had sought it, on the footing that it was the wrong remedy, and awarded Rs. 5 lakh under Article 142 as full and final settlement.

Read together, these decisions put three questions to the trial court: first, whether the cause of action for the omitted claim existed when the earlier proceeding was instituted; second, whether the party was obliged, in the circumstances of that proceeding, to assert it in order to maintain or defend his position; and third, whether the claim is in substance the same as the one decided, a negative answer to either of the first two questions being sufficient to defeat Explanation IV.

# 4. Where the Two Provisions Meet

The two provisions collide when a defendant asks for rejection of the plaint under clause (d) on the ground that an earlier suit or decree bars it, whether by Section 11 or by Order II Rule 2, and the Supreme Court's answer to this has been consistent for decades and was restated this year, as the following authorities show.

| Authority | Proposition |
|:-------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------|
| *Gurbux Singh v. Bhooralal*, AIR 1964 SC 1810 | A plea under Order II Rule 2 requires the defendant to prove that the later suit is on the same cause of action, and to produce the plaint of the earlier suit. |
| *V. Rajeshwari v. T.C. Saravanabava*, (2004) 1 SCC 551 | Identity of causes of action is a matter for trial, where the earlier pleadings are studied. |
| *Srihari Hanumandas Totala v. Hemant Vithal Kamat*, (2021) 9 SCC 99 | Res judicata is a mixed question of law and fact and cannot be decided on an application under Rule 11(d). |
| *Keshav Sood v. Kirti Pradeep Sood*, C.A. No. 5841 of 2023 | The earlier pleadings and judgment must be examined, so the plea cannot be decided at the threshold. |
| *Pandurangan v. T. Jayarama Chettiar*, 2025 INSC 825 | A plea based on an earlier ex parte decree, with collusion and fraud alleged, cannot be decided on the assertions in the application. |
| *S. Valliammai v. S. Ramanathan*, 2026 INSC 372 | Order II Rule 2 cannot be a ground for rejection under Rule 11(d). |

The reasoning behind these decisions is structural, for clause (d) applies where the plaint itself shows a bar created by express or implied law, a statutory prohibition such as Section 34 of the SARFAESI Act being the paradigm case, whereas Order II Rule 2 and Section 11 are different in kind, because each can be applied only by comparing the present plaint with the pleadings, issues and decision of another suit and sometimes with evidence as well. In *S. Valliammai*, for example, the Madras High Court had rejected the plaint in a second suit seeking a declaration that a power of attorney was void by setting it beside the first suit,[^19] and the Supreme Court (Nagarathna and Bhuyan JJ.) restored the trial court's order, holding that although Order II Rule 2 may defeat a suit after trial it is not a ground for summary rejection.

*Pandurangan* goes one step further, for the plaintiff there alleged that an earlier ex parte decree was collusive and did not bind him, and Narasimha and Bagchi JJ. held that the plea of res judicata could not be decided merely on the assertions in the defendant's application,[^20] restoring the suit of 2009 and expressly keeping every defence open for final determination.

The consequence for procedure is therefore clear: a defendant with a genuine plea of res judicata or of Order II Rule 2 should not frame it as an application under Rule 11 but should plead it in the written statement, claim a specific issue and prove the record of the earlier suit, since Order XIV Rule 2(2) permits an issue to be tried first only where it relates to jurisdiction or to a bar created by law, and a plea of res judicata ordinarily needs the earlier record before it can be decided.[^21]

# 5. Recommended Procedure in the Trial Court

## 5.1 Position of the defendant

The defendant should begin by deciding which clause is being relied upon, because clauses (a) and (d) must be demonstrable from the plaint and its annexures and, if the earlier judgment or any document of the defendant's own is needed to show the bar, Rule 11 is the wrong vehicle; the defendant should next identify every defendant and relief affected, since an objection that does not reach the whole plaint will fail however strong it is against part, and the application should then cite paragraphs and annexures of the plaint with dates and, where limitation is raised, show from the plaintiff's own pleading both the date on which the cause of action accrued and the date of institution.

Timing matters as well, for although the power is available at any stage an application made after the plaintiff's evidence has closed invites costs, as it did in *Nirav Madhukar Buch*, and admissions in cross-examination should not be relied upon because they are evidence and Rule 11 excludes evidence; finally, res judicata and Order II Rule 2 should be pleaded in the written statement, with an issue claimed and certified copies of the earlier plaint, written statement, issues and judgment obtained, so that the burden identified in *Gurbux Singh* is discharged.

## 5.2 Position of the plaintiff

The plaintiff, for his part, should state the date and event giving rise to each cause of action and keep the pleading consistent with the annexures, because the annexures will be read against him, and where limitation depends on knowledge the facts of concealment or late discovery should be pleaded with particularity, since a bare assertion will be treated as the illusion that *Arivandandam* warns against. Similarly, where an earlier proceeding exists the plaint should explain why the present relief was not and could not have been claimed there and should identify any cause of action that arose later, as in *Ashok Kumar* and *Makardhwaj Ram*, while admissions on the face of the plaint that establish a bar should be avoided; and in resisting the application the plaintiff should take the narrow ground first, namely that the enquiry is confined to the plaint, and only then take the court to the authorities discussed in Part 4.

## 5.3 Position of the court

For the court, the sequence of enquiry is short, since it must ask whether the application is confined to the plaint and its documents, whether the bar appears without evidence, whether the objection reaches the whole plaint and whether the application is timely, and if the answer to any of these is no the application should be dismissed and the plea left to be tried as an issue, whereas if every answer is yes the court has no option but to reject the plaint, because the provision is mandatory.

# 6. Conclusion

Order VII Rule 11 and Section 11 are both instruments of finality, but they operate at different points, the first examining a single document and rejecting an unmaintainable suit before it consumes a trial and the second examining another proceeding and preventing a decided matter from being reopened, so that advocates lose time when they ask the first to do the work of the second. The authorities discussed here yield a simple rule of thumb, which is that if the bar can be shown only by going outside the plaint, the plea belongs in the written statement and at trial and not in an application at the threshold.
